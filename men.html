Men's Health


Being healthy is about more than looking strong or pushing through the day. It means taking care of your mind, fuelling your body, staying active, and knowing when to seek medical advice. Yet, men can sometimes put their health aside by ignoring warning signs, struggling in silence, or waiting until a problem becomes harder to manage. Your health deserves attention before something goes wrong. Explore this section to discover practical ways to improve your mental wellbeing, build strength, make healthier food choices, and take charge of your long-term health. Small changes today can help lay the foundation for a healthier future.

Mental Health

Mental health affects how we think, feel, handle stress, and connect with others. Men can experience depression, anxiety, and other mental health conditions, but expectations to appear strong and self-reliant may discourage them from expressing their struggles or seeking help. Ignoring mental health problems can affect relationships, work, and daily life. Men are also more likely than women to die by suicide, making early recognition and support especially important.


Recognize the Signs

Mental health struggles do not always appear as sadness. Common signs include:
  • Persistent stress, worry, or hopelessness.
  • Increased irritability, anger, or aggression.
  • Changes in sleep, appetite, or energy.
  • Withdrawing from others or losing interest in activities.
  • Increased alcohol, drug use, or risky behaviour.
  • Difficulty managing daily responsibilities.

So, What Can You Do?

  • Acknowledge your feelings. Recognize when stress or emotional difficulties persist instead of dismissing them.
  • Talk to someone. Share your concerns with a trusted friend, family member, or partner.
  • Develop healthier coping habits. Avoid relying on alcohol or drugs to manage difficult emotions.
  • Seek professional help. A doctor or qualified mental health professional can help you understand your symptoms and find appropriate support.
  • Support other men. Listen without judgment and encourage friends who seem to be struggling to seek help.
Remember, being strong does not mean handling everything alone. Recognizing when you need support and taking action are important parts of maintaining your health.

Fitness & Wellness

Exercise is not just about appearance or building muscle. Regular physical activity supports heart health, helps manage blood pressure and blood sugar, strengthens bones, and improves physical function. It can also support mood, reduce stress, and improve sleep. Over time, staying active may help lower the risk of certain chronic diseases. For men, fitness is an important part of long-term health. Its benefits extend beyond the gym, helping maintain strength, mobility, and independence as the body ages.


Find the Right Balance
A well-rounded fitness routine includes more than one type of exercise.
Cardio - Activities such as brisk walking, cycling, swimming, or running challenge the heart and lungs and support cardiovascular fitness.
Strength Training - Weightlifting, push-ups, and other resistance exercises help build or maintain muscle strength. Research also links strength training with better physical function and long-term health outcomes.
Balance and Mobility - Exercises that challenge balance and maintain movement control can help support stability and reduce the risk of falls, particularly as people get older.

You do not need an overly complicated workout plan. Circuit training is one option for combining several exercises into an efficient session. Alternatively, a full-body routine or a split routine that trains different muscle groups on different days can both be reasonable approaches. A study comparing split and full-body training in previously untrained participants found improvements in strength and muscle size with both approaches. The best choice depends on your schedule, goals, and what you can maintain consistently.

How Much is Enough?
Consistency matters more than following a complicated plan. Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health reported that a meta-analysis found an association between 30-60 minutes of weekly strength training and a 10-20% lower risk of premature death during the study periods. Grip strength offers one example of how physical strength relates to wider health. Research has associated stronger grip with measures such as overall strength, bone density, and mobility in later life. A practical starting point is to include strength exercises for the major muscle groups at least twice a week, alongside regular aerobic activity. Start at a level suited to your current fitness and increase the challenge gradually as your ability improves. Choose activities you enjoy, set realistic goals, and give your body time to recover. Pay attention to discomfort and fatigue rather than forcing yourself through every session. A routine that is manageable and repeatable is more useful over time than an intense plan you cannot sustain.

Nutrition

Food does more than satisfy hunger. It provides the energy and nutrients your body needs to maintain muscle, support normal hormone function, protect your heart, and keep your body working properly. Over time, poor eating habits can increase the risk of conditions such as heart disease, type 2 diabetes, and certain cancers. Nutrition can also influence male reproductive health, including sperm quality.
Eat a variety of foods. Include vegetables, fruits, whole grains, beans, and other fibre rich foods. These provide essential vitamins, minerals, and fibre that support digestion and overall health.

Include protein. Eggs, fish, lean meats, tofu, beans, lentils, and dairy products provide protein needed for maintaining and repairing body tissues.
Choose healthy fats. Nuts, seeds, avocados, olive oil, and oily fish provide unsaturated fats. Dietary fats are also involved in normal body functions, including hormone production.
Limit excess salt, added sugar, and saturated fat. Reduce frequent consumption of highly processed snacks, sugary drinks, and foods high in salt or saturated fat. Moderation is more sustainable than trying to eliminate every food you enjoy.
Drink enough water. Water supports normal body functions and helps maintain hydration throughout the day.

Nutrition and Male Fertility
Diet may also influence reproductive health. Research suggests that dietary patterns rich in plant foods and fish are associated with better indicators of sperm quality. Unsaturated fats, particularly omega-3 fatty acids, may support sperm function, while excessive saturated fat intake and an imbalance of dietary fats may have unfavourable effects.

Health Essentials

Feeling healthy does not always mean everything is fine. High blood pressure, high cholesterol, and diabetes can develop without obvious symptoms. Preventive health checks help identify potential problems early, giving you and your doctor an opportunity to discuss the next steps before complications develop. Yet many men delay medical appointments because they fear receiving bad news, feel uncomfortable discussing personal health concerns, or believe they only need help when something feels wrong. Research on men’s screening behaviour has identified these concerns, along with limited time and low awareness, as common barriers to preventive care.



Six Areas of Mens Health to Monitor
The health checks you need depend on your age, medical history, family history, and individual risk factors. These are six areas worth discussing with a healthcare professional.
  1. Routine Primary care visits
    2. A regular appointment gives your doctor an overview of your health. Depending on your needs, this may include checking your blood pressure, weight, lifestyle habits, and family history. It also provides an opportunity to discuss concerns before they become more serious.
  2. Blood tests
    3. Blood tests may assess cholesterol, blood sugar, blood cell counts, and other indicators of health. Which tests you need, and how often, depends on your risk factors and your doctor’s assessment.
  3. Colorectal cancer screening
    4. Screening for colorectal cancer can help detect disease or precancerous changes. The NewYork-Presbyterian article discusses screening from age 45 for people at average risk, with earlier assessment potentially appropriate for those with a family history. The suitable method and timing should be discussed with a healthcare professional.
  4. Prostate health assessment
    5. Prostate cancer screening may involve a prostate-specific antigen (PSA) blood test. However, screening has potential benefits and risks, including false alarms and unnecessary follow-up procedures. Rather than assuming every man needs the same test at the same age, discuss your personal risk and the pros and cons with your doctor.
  5. Skin health checks
    6. Changes in moles, new growths, or unusual skin lesions deserve medical attention. Your risk may be higher if you have a family history of skin cancer or substantial sun exposure. Ask a healthcare professional whether a routine skin examination is appropriate for you.
  6. Cardiovascular health
    7. Heart health deserves attention throughout adulthood, particularly if you have high blood pressure, high cholesterol, diabetes, a family history of heart disease, or other risk factors. Your doctor can determine whether additional assessment or specialist care is necessary.

You do not need to wait for symptoms before talking to a doctor. Keep track of your medical and family history, ask which preventive checks are appropriate for your age, and follow up on recommended appointments.
